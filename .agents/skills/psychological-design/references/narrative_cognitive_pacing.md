# Narrative & Cognitive Pacing — Psychological Design Reference

A technical reference mapping how prose and story structure manipulate reader attention, cognitive load, expectation framing, and scene-level value shifts. Designed for cross-referencing with `comedy-tv-writer`, `poetry-craft`, `ai-video-storytelling`, and `book-to-video-prompt`.

## 1. Expectation, Attention & Identification (The Three Levers)

Every psychologically designed piece of writing operates on three core cognitive levers:

```
                  ┌───────────────────────────────┐
                  │      PSYCHOLOGICAL LEVERS     │
                  └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                                  │
      ┌───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┐
      ▼                           ▼                           ▼
┌──────────────┐          ┌──────────────┐          ┌──────────────┐
│ EXPECTATION  │          │  ATTENTION   │          │IDENTIFICATION│
│ (What the    │          │ (What's in   │          │(Degree of    │
│ reader       │          │ focus vs.    │          │ emotional    │
│ predicts)    │          │ subtext)     │          │ self-projection)
└──────────────┘          └──────────────┘          └──────────────┘
```

1. **Expectation:** Building a predictive schema in the reader's working memory, then either fulfilling it (payoff), delaying it (suspense), subverting it (surprise), or shattering it (revelation).
2. **Attention:** Managing cognitive load and focus — controlling what the reader is looking at directly vs. what they are tracking in the peripheral subtext.
3. **Identification:** Calibrating how closely the reader mirrors a character's internal state vs. observing them with ironic distance.

## 2. Scene-Level Value Shifts (Robert McKee Model)

A scene is not a collection of events; it is a unit of conflict that changes a character's story condition from one emotional/existential state to another.

### The Value-Shift Mechanism
Every effective scene moves between opposing poles along a specific **value axis**:
- *Hope → Despair*
- *Status / Control → Vulnerability / Exposure*
- *Innocence → Disillusionment*
- *Connection → Isolation*

### The Audience Pre-Awareness Principle (McKee)
The audience senses the value shift *before* the character states it aloud. 
- If a character says "I'm ruined" at the exact moment the value turns, the line feels redundant.
- If the scene's physical action and subtext demonstrate the value shift, the character's line can be restrained or ironic, creating a sharp gap between **affect** and **stakes**.

### The Scale Mismatch Technique
A major psychological lever in both comedy and drama:
* **Drama:** Treating an enormous catastrophic event with quiet, minimal understatement (e.g. Ernest Hemingway, Raymond Carver). The reader's cognitive system fills in the suppressed emotion, making it hit twice as hard.
* **Comedy:** Treating a trivial, low-stakes inconvenience as a life-or-death crisis (e.g. John Vorhaus's *Comic Flaw*, Fawlty Towers), or conversely, treating a collapse with total calm (e.g. Cuzzy Obvious / Uncle Obvious).

## 3. The Gap Between Said & Meant (Subtext & Cognitive Syntax)

Steven Pinker (*The Sense of Style*) and Robert McKee both emphasize that human cognition is tuned to process **subtext** — inferring indirect intent beneath direct language.

### Why Direct Statements Fail Cognitive Engagement
When a character states their internal state directly (*"I am angry and hurt"*), the reader's inferential engine shuts down because no cognitive decoding is required. The text becomes flat.

### The Three Layers of Subtext
1. **Text:** The literal words spoken or described.
2. **Subtext:** The actual desire, fear, or status claim driving the words.
3. **Context / Environment:** The physical surroundings and unspoken history shaping the interaction.

### Cognitive Syntax (Steven Pinker)
Syntax controls the order of visual mental simulation:
* **Front-Loading (Topic First):** Establishes the mental container before filling it with details (*"In the center of the flooded yard, a lone galvanized bucket floated..."*).
* **Branching & Nesting:** High cognitive load occurs when clauses interrupt the subject-verb relationship. Use nested clauses deliberately to evoke disorientation or overthinking; use short, crisp subject-verb-object structures for sudden impact.

## 4. Environment-Reactive Mood Shaping

Setting is not passive background scenery; it is a cognitive calibration frame.

### The Mechanism of Context Re-Calibration
The exact same dialogue delivered in a sterile hospital hallway, a sunlit coastal shed, or a cramped kitchen carries radically different psychological weight.
- **Environment as Mirror:** The setting externalizes the character's internal tension (e.g. Otaki shed dust motes and lawnmower fumes grounding cosmic physics).
- **Environment as Friction:** The setting resists the character's goals (e.g. bad phone reception, loud cicadas, cold rain on iron roof), forcing them to struggle against physical reality while trying to communicate.

## 5. Somatic Restraint, Conversational Latency & Metaphoric Objective Correlative

### The Cognitive Law of Silence
When dialogue pauses, cognitive inference activates. An audience passively receives spoken exposition; they actively decode silence.

1. **Conversational Latency (The Processing Beat):**
   - In realistic human interaction, major emotional truths are not met with instant rejoinders. There is an unhurried 1.5s–3.0s processing gap where the nervous system absorbs the impact.
   - Status is revealed by who can endure the silence. High-status figures let the silence build gravity; low-status figures panic and over-explain.

2. **Somatic Restraint (The Pressure-Vessel Model):**
   - The audience does not mirror melodramatic histrionics (loud crying, theatrical scowls); they empathize with **containment**—the visible effort *not* to lose control.
   - Somatic tells leak the truth: masseter clench, delayed glottal swallow, unmoving spine, eyes tracking down to a mundane surface.

3. **Metaphoric Displacement (The Objective Correlative):**
   - Emotion too large to speak directly is displaced onto concrete domestic objects (a cooling cup of tea, a receipt folded obsessively, an unlit cigarette).
   - In poetry and screenwriting, the object becomes the psychological heat sink. The metaphor carries the emotional charge without verbal assertion.
