# Poetic Tension Mechanics — Psychological Design Reference

A technical reference mapping the psychological mechanisms that make poetry *do something* to a reader's nervous system — the craft of manipulating attention, withholding, and emotional reorientation at the line level. Designed for cross-referencing with `poetry-craft`.

---

## 1. The Volta (The Psychological Pivot)

The volta is the moment a poem *turns* — the reader is oriented one way and then the ground shifts. It is the poetic equivalent of the joke's punchline, but the payoff is recognition or emotional recalibration rather than laughter.

### Classic Volta Placements
| Form | Traditional Volta Position | Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Petrarchan Sonnet** | Line 9 (the turn from octave to sestet) | Problem → Resolution, or Observation → Reinterpretation |
| **Shakespearean Sonnet** | Line 13 (the couplet) | 12 lines of argument → sudden compression or reversal |
| **Free Verse** | Variable — often 2/3 through the poem | The reader has settled in; the turn unsettles them |
| **Haiku** | The kireji (cutting word) between lines 2 and 3 | Concrete image → leap to larger resonance |

### The Cognitive Mechanism
The volta exploits the brain's **predictive coding** — the same mechanism as humor's incongruity resolution, but operating at a slower emotional frequency. The reader builds a mental model across the first section of the poem, and the volta forces a rapid model-update. The emotional charge comes from the *speed* of the reorientation:
* A gradual shift feels like deepening understanding.
* A sudden pivot feels like revelation or vertigo.

### Volta Types (Emotional Signatures)
| Type | Movement | Emotional Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Contradictory** | Assertion → Negation ("But...") | Surprise, dramatic irony, dismantling |
| **Concessive** | Opposition → Acceptance ("And yet...") | Grief, resignation, complex maturity |
| **Expansive** | Particular → Universal ("All of this...") | Awe, cosmic perspective shift |
| **Reductive** | Grand → Intimate ("But here, tonight...") | Tenderness, grounding, earned simplicity |
| **Retrospective** | Present → Memory ("I remember when...") | Nostalgia, loss, temporal vertigo |

## 2. Enjambment as Suspense

Enjambment (running a sentence across a line break without punctuation) is the poet's primary tool for manipulating the reader's *temporal experience* of meaning.

### How It Works Psychologically
At every line break, the reader's brain makes a **micro-prediction** about what comes next. Enjambment exploits this by:
1. **Completing one meaning at the line end** (the reader briefly holds this reading).
2. **Overriding it at the start of the next line** (the continuation reframes the meaning).

The reader experiences a brief cognitive double-exposure — two meanings superimposed for a fraction of a second.

### Enjambment Types & Tension Levels
| Type | Example | Tension Level |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mild (syntactic continuation)** | "I walked across the / empty field at dawn" | Low — expected continuation |
| **Moderate (delayed object)** | "She held the / nothing he had left behind" | Medium — "held the what?" creates momentary suspense |
| **Aggressive (reframing)** | "I wanted to die / my hair a different colour" | High — first line reads as a complete (devastating) statement before the next line reframes it |
| **Staircase (cascading)** | Three+ consecutive enjambed lines building momentum | Propulsive — the reader is pulled forward without rest |

### The Counter-Tool: End-Stopped Lines
End-stopped lines (syntactically complete, ending with punctuation) create **weight, finality, and breathing room**. The interplay between enjambed and end-stopped lines is how a poet controls pacing — the equivalent of a film editor's cuts.

## 3. Concrete Image → Abstract Leap

The most reliable move in poetry: ground the reader in something they can *see, hear, touch, taste, or smell*, then ask them to feel something bigger.

### The Cognitive Sequence
1. **Sensory anchoring:** The concrete image activates the reader's embodied simulation circuits (mirror neurons, sensory cortex). They're *in* the scene.
2. **Emotional transfer:** Because the reader is sensorily present, any abstraction that follows inherits the image's weight. The reader doesn't process the abstraction as a claim to evaluate — they process it as an experience to feel.
3. **The failure mode (and why it matters):** Starting with the abstraction ("Love is eternal") gives the reader nothing to anchor to. The claim floats. Starting with the image ("The coffee ring on the table where you sat") earns the right to the feeling.

### Mary Oliver's Economy Principle
Mary Oliver's craft rule: **the image should do the emotional work so the poet doesn't have to explain it.** If the image is precise enough, the abstraction becomes unnecessary — the reader supplies the feeling themselves, and self-generated emotion is always stronger than stated emotion.

### The Uncle Obvious Application
This same principle powers the best moments in the Uncle Obvious narrative: the concrete, sensory detail of the Otaki shed (macrocarpa dust, chipped enamel mug, lawnmower fumes) grounds the reader so firmly in the physical world that the deadpan "science" feels warm and embodied rather than merely wrong. The gap between the shed's humble reality and the cosmic physics being explained *is* the image-to-abstraction leap — just played for comedy rather than lyric emotion.

## 4. Repetition & Refrain as Pressure

When a line or phrase repeats in a poem, it means something different each time — because the reader's context has shifted.

### The Cognitive Mechanism: Context-Dependent Reprocessing
The brain doesn't store repeated stimuli as identical copies. Each encounter is processed against the *current* emotional and informational context. A refrain that first sounds like a statement of fact can, by its fourth repetition, sound like a prayer, a threat, or a lament — without changing a single word.

### Repetition Strategies
| Strategy | Mechanism | Example Forms |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Verbatim refrain** | Identical words, shifting meaning through accumulated context | Villanelle (A1 and A2 refrains), Pantoum (chain-repeated lines), Rondeau (R-line) |
| **Anaphora** | Repeated opening phrase across lines/stanzas ("I have…", "We are…") | Free verse, spoken word, Biblical parallelism |
| **Incremental repetition** | A phrase repeated with one element changed each time | Ballad tradition, folk song, escalation comedy |
| **Echo / near-repetition** | The phrase returns slightly altered — the difference *is* the meaning | Duplex form, Ghazal couplets |

### The Pressure Effect
Each repetition tightens the emotional ratchet. The reader cannot hear the refrain innocently anymore — it carries the accumulated weight of everything that has happened between repetitions. This is why the villanelle is such an effective grief form: the repeated lines become inescapable, mirroring the obsessive return of loss.
